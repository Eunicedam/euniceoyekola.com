How I cut cost per order by 90% in 4 weeks with Meta Ads

A UK pet treats brand went from £164.65 to £15.81 per order. Here is exactly what we did, including the parts that didn't work.

When this UK pet treats brand started running Meta Ads with me, the first week was expensive. Each order cost £164.65. Four weeks later, with roughly the same weekly spend, an order cost £15.81. That's a drop of about 90%.

Here is exactly what happened, including the parts that didn't work, because I think honest numbers are more useful than a highlight reel.

The results, week by week

  • Week 1: 3 orders, at £164.65 per order
  • Week 2: 26 orders, at £32.46 per order
  • Week 3: 38 orders, at £21.39 per order
  • Week 4: 56 orders, at £15.81 per order, with a 1.51 return on ad spend

The brand's break-even point was a return of 1.44, so by week 4 the ads were paying for themselves and more. Across the whole month there were 125 orders and £3,130 in sales from ads.

Step 1: a planned testing phase

The first two weeks were for testing, and we agreed that before spending anything. We ran different videos, images and audiences side by side to find out what actually sells, rather than guessing.

This is the part many businesses skip. They launch one ad, it doesn't work in three days, and they give up on Meta Ads completely. Testing costs something, but it's how you find the ad that pays for everything else.

Step 2: we found a winning ad

One creative, a bundle offer, clearly beat the rest. It brought in 63 orders from £700.51 of spend, a 2.20 return on ad spend, with a 9.65% click rate. That one ad brought in about half of all the month's orders.

Step 3: budget moved to what worked

Once we could see the winner, we switched off the ads and audiences that weren't selling and moved the budget behind the bundle ad and its audience. The traffic got cheaper and better at the same time:

  • Cost per click fell from £0.42 to £0.22
  • Click rate rose from 5.5% to 7.6%
  • Weekly add-to-carts went from 73 to 264

The honest part

Across the full month, the return on ad spend was 1.01, roughly break-even. That's because the first two weeks were spent learning. If you only judge an account on its first fortnight, you would have stopped too early and never found the ad that made weeks 3 and 4 profitable.

What you can take from this

  • Plan for a testing phase, and agree a budget for it in advance.
  • Test real differences: offers, formats and angles, not just small colour changes.
  • When a winner appears, be ruthless. Turn off what isn't working.
  • Judge results week by week, not only by the monthly total.
